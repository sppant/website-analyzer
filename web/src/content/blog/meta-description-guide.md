The meta description is the `<meta name="description">` tag in your page's `<head>`. Google often uses it as the snippet — the grey text under the blue title in search results. It is **not a ranking factor**, and Google confirmed that years ago. But the snippet is frequently what decides whether someone clicks your result or a competitor's, so it affects traffic indirectly through click-through rate.

## Ideal length

-   **Characters:** aim for roughly **120–160**. Below ~70 you are wasting the space; above ~160 the end gets cut off.
-   **Pixels:** Google actually truncates by **width, not character count** — around **920px on desktop**, and less on mobile. Capital letters and wide characters spend the budget faster, so a description of 155 lowercase characters may fit where 150 characters full of capitals and "W"s does not.
-   In practice: write about 150 characters, put the important part first, and preview it. Our [SERP preview tool](/serp-preview) shows the pixel width and where the snippet will cut off, on both desktop and mobile.

## How to write one that earns the click

1.  **Answer the search, briefly.** If the query is "how to descale a kettle", the description should promise the answer — not describe your company.
2.  **Front-load the value.** The first ~120 characters are what always show.
3.  **Use the key terms naturally.** Google bolds words that match the query, which draws the eye. Do not stuff them; write for the person.
4.  **Give a reason to choose you** — "free", "no signup", "step by step", "with examples", a specific number.
5.  **Match the page.** A description that oversells earns the click and an instant bounce, which helps no one.
6.  **Make every one unique.** Duplicate descriptions across pages are a wasted opportunity and one of the most common audit findings.
7.  **Avoid quotation marks** in the raw HTML — they can truncate the attribute — and skip decorative punctuation.

## Why Google rewrites meta descriptions

Google generates its own snippet **more than half the time**, especially for longer-tail queries, because it tries to show text that matches the specific search. This is normal and is not a penalty. You are more likely to keep the description you wrote when:

-   it clearly summarises the whole page
-   the query closely matches the page's main topic — a brand or product name, for example
-   the page does not contain an obviously more relevant passage for that query

You cannot force it. Write a strong description for your primary query and accept that Google will substitute its own for the long tail.

## Do you need one on every page?

-   **Important pages** — homepage, key landing pages, cornerstone content — yes, hand-write them.
-   **Large sets of similar pages** — products, listings — use a good templated pattern such as `{Product} — {key attribute} — {benefit}. {shipping or returns line}.` It beats leaving them blank or duplicating one description everywhere.
-   **Thin or utility pages you have `noindex`ed** — do not bother.

Leaving the tag blank is a valid choice; Google will always generate something. But you give up the chance to influence the snippet on the queries that matter most.

## Common mistakes

-   Duplicating the [title tag](/blog/title-tag-seo) word for word.
-   Writing for a keyword instead of a reader.
-   Running past ~160 characters so the call to action is the part that gets cut.
-   Letting the CMS fall back to the first 160 characters of body text — often a nav menu or boilerplate.
-   Never checking what actually renders in the search result.
